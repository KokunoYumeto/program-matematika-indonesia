# Lecture 30: Bézout's Theorem {#br-ak-2012-l30}

In this lecture we prove Bézout's Theorem for the projective plane. It states that, for two projective curves in the projective plane with no common component and of degrees $m$ and $n$, respectively, the sum of all their intersection multiplicities equals $mn$. Our presentation largely follows Fulton's treatment.

## Homogeneous components of a fixed degree {#br-ak-2012-l30-s01}

<!-- upstream_entity: Projektive ebene Kurven/Satz von Bézout/Beweisaufbau/Textabschnitt -->

For a polynomial ring $P$ and a natural number $\ell$, we shall write $P_\ell$ for the *homogeneous component of degree $\ell$*, the space of all homogeneous polynomials of degree $\ell$. We use the same notation for graded quotients of a polynomial ring, that is, quotients by homogeneous ideals. As a vector space over the base field $K$, this component is generated by all monomials of degree $\ell$. In particular, $P_\ell$ is a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space.

<!-- upstream_entity: Schnitttheorie von Kurven/Satz von Bézout/Dimension von Stufe im homogenen Restklassenring/Fakt -->

### Lemma 30.1: dimension of a homogeneous component of the quotient ring {#br-ak-2012-l30-lem-01}

Let $K$ be a field, let

$$
F,G\in K[X,Y,Z]=P
$$

be homogeneous polynomials of degrees $m$ and $n$, and suppose that $F$ and $G$ have no nonconstant common divisor. Then

$$
\dim_K\bigl(P/(F,G)\bigr)_\ell=mn
$$

for sufficiently large $\ell$.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2012-l30-lem-01-proof}

Consider the exact sequence

$$
0\longrightarrow P
\stackrel{\begin{pmatrix}G\\-F\end{pmatrix}}{\longrightarrow}P\times P
\stackrel{(F,G)}{\longrightarrow}P
\longrightarrow P/(F,G)\longrightarrow0.
$$

The first map is

$$
H\longmapsto(GH,-FH),
$$

the next map is

$$
(A,B)\longmapsto AF+BG,
$$

and the last map takes residue classes. All these maps are $P$-module homomorphisms. Injectivity at the first term is clear because $P$ is an integral domain. The sequence is clearly exact at the last two terms; only exactness at the second term remains to be proved.

The composition of the first two maps is zero. Conversely, suppose

$$
AF+BG=0
$$

in $P$. Since $P$ is a unique factorisation domain and $F,G$ are coprime, $A$ must be a multiple of $G$. Write $A=QG$. The equation above becomes

$$
G(QF+B)=0,
$$

so $B=-QF$. Thus

$$
(A,B)=Q(G,-F)
$$

comes from the map on the left.

> **Source correction AGC-CORR-0130 - sign in the relation.** The source says that $B$ is a multiple of $F$ “with the same factor”, but the sign imposed by $AF+BG=0$ gives $B=-QF$. This edition displays the minus sign explicitly, in agreement with the map $H\mapsto(GH,-FH)$ already printed in the source.

Since $F$ and $G$ are homogeneous of fixed degrees, the sequence restricts to homogeneous components. This gives the exact sequence

$$
0\longrightarrow P_{\ell-m-n}
\stackrel{\begin{pmatrix}G\\-F\end{pmatrix}}{\longrightarrow}
P_{\ell-m}\times P_{\ell-n}
\stackrel{(F,G)}{\longrightarrow}P_\ell
\longrightarrow\bigl(P/(F,G)\bigr)_\ell\longrightarrow0,
$$

where components with negative indices are defined to be $0$. The restricted sequence remains exact because the homogeneous components of a homogeneous homomorphism do not mix. All the components involved are now finite-dimensional vector spaces.

If $\ell\geq m+n$, all indices are nonnegative, and

$$
\dim_K(P_\ell)=\frac{(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}2.
$$

By additivity of vector-space dimension in exact complexes (see Exercise 14.7), we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim_K\bigl((P/(F,G))_\ell\bigr)
&=\frac{(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}2
-\frac{(\ell-m+1)(\ell-m+2)}2\\
&\quad-\frac{(\ell-n+1)(\ell-n+2)}2
+\frac{(\ell-m-n+1)(\ell-m-n+2)}2\\
&=\frac{2-(-m+1)(-m+2)-(-n+1)(-n+2)
+(-m-n+1)(-m-n+2)}2\\
&=\frac{2mn}{2}\\
&=mn.
\end{aligned}
$$

$\square$

## Injectivity of multiplication by $Z$ {#br-ak-2012-l30-s02}

<!-- upstream_entity: Schnitttheorie von Kurven/Satz von Bézout/Injektivität der Multiplikation mit Z im homogenen Restklassenring/Fakt -->

### Lemma 30.2: multiplication by $Z$ in the quotient ring {#br-ak-2012-l30-lem-02}

Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field and let

$$
F,G\in K[X,Y,Z]
$$

be homogeneous polynomials with no common projective zero on

$$
V_+(Z)\subseteq\mathbb P_K^2.
$$

Write the corresponding quotient ring as

$$
R=K[X,Y,Z]/(F,G).
$$

Then the map

$$
\begin{aligned}
R&\longrightarrow R,\\
H&\longmapsto ZH
\end{aligned}
$$

is injective.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2012-l30-lem-02-proof}

Take $H\in K[X,Y,Z]$ and suppose its class maps to $0$. This means there are $L,M\in K[X,Y,Z]$ such that

$$
ZH=LF+MG.
$$

Substitute $Z=0$ in this equation. In $K[X,Y]$, we obtain

$$
0=L(X,Y,0)F(X,Y,0)+M(X,Y,0)G(X,Y,0).
$$

Since $F$ and $G$ have no common projective zero on $V_+(Z)$, the polynomials $F(X,Y,0)$ and $G(X,Y,0)$ have only $(0,0)$ as a common zero in $\mathbb A_K^2$. They are therefore coprime in $K[X,Y]$. Hence there is a $Q\in K[X,Y]$ with

$$
L(X,Y,0)=QG(X,Y,0),
\qquad
M(X,Y,0)=-QF(X,Y,0).
$$

Lifting back to $K[X,Y,Z]$, this means

$$
L=QG(X,Y,0)+Z\overline L,
\qquad
M=-QF(X,Y,0)+Z\overline M
$$

for some $\overline L,\overline M\in K[X,Y,Z]$. Writing

$$
F=F(X,Y,0)+Z\overline F,
\qquad
G=G(X,Y,0)+Z\overline G,
$$

the original equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
ZH
&=LF+MG\\
&=\bigl(QG(X,Y,0)+Z\overline L\bigr)F
+\bigl(-QF(X,Y,0)+Z\overline M\bigr)G\\
&=Q\bigl(G-Z\overline G\bigr)F
-Q\bigl(F-Z\overline F\bigr)G
+Z\overline L F+Z\overline M G\\
&=-QZ\overline G F+QZ\overline F G
+Z\overline L F+Z\overline M G\\
&=Z\bigl(-Q\overline G F+Q\overline F G
+\overline L F+\overline M G\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

We can cancel $Z$ from this equation in the polynomial ring, obtaining an expression for $H$ as a linear combination of $F$ and $G$. Thus the class of $H$ in $R$ is also $0$. $\square$

## Bézout's Theorem {#br-ak-2012-l30-s03}

We now come to Bézout's Theorem.

<!-- upstream_entity: Schnitttheorie von Kurven/Satz von Bézout/Fakt -->

### Theorem 30.3: Bézout's Theorem {#br-ak-2012-l30-thm-01}

Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field and let

$$
F,G\in K[X,Y,Z]
$$

be homogeneous polynomials of degrees $m$ and $n$ with no common component, with corresponding curves

$$
C=V_+(F),\qquad D=V_+(G)\subseteq\mathbb P_K^2.
$$

Then

$$
\sum_P\operatorname{mult}_P(C,D)=mn.
$$

#### Proof {#br-ak-2012-l30-thm-01-proof}

The intersection $C\cap D$ consists of only finitely many points. By Exercise 27.10, after a projective change of coordinates we may assume that all intersection points lie in

$$
\mathbb A_K^2=D_+(Z)\subseteq\mathbb P_K^2.
$$

Let $\widetilde F,\widetilde G\in K[X,Y]$ be the dehomogenisations describing the affine curves $C\cap\mathbb A_K^2$ and $D\cap\mathbb A_K^2$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{P\in\mathbb P_K^2}\operatorname{mult}_P(F,G)
&=\sum_{P\in\mathbb A_K^2}
\operatorname{mult}_P(\widetilde F,\widetilde G)\\
&=\sum_{P\in\mathbb A_K^2}
\dim_K\!\left(
K[X,Y]_{\mathfrak m_P}/(\widetilde F,\widetilde G)
\right)\\
&=\dim_K\!\left(K[X,Y]/(\widetilde F,\widetilde G)\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The last equality follows from Theorem 26.11. We shall relate the $K$-dimension of this inhomogeneous quotient ring to the dimension of a homogeneous component of the quotient ring

$$
\bigl(K[X,Y,Z]/(F,G)\bigr)_\ell.
$$

By Lemma 30.1, for sufficiently large $\ell$ the latter component has dimension $mn$.

Choose a basis

$$
V_1,\ldots,V_{mn}
$$

of $\bigl(K[X,Y,Z]/(F,G)\bigr)_\ell$, with $\ell$ sufficiently large and fixed. We claim that the dehomogenisations

$$
v_i=V_i(X,Y,1),\qquad i=1,\ldots,mn,
$$

form a basis of

$$
K[X,Y]/(\widetilde F,\widetilde G).
$$

First we prove that these elements generate. Take any $q\in K[X,Y]$ and let its homogenisation

$$
Q\in K[X,Y,Z]
$$

have degree $d$. Choose an integer $e\geq0$ such that $d+e\geq\ell$. By Lemma 30.2, for every $\lambda\geq1$ the map

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bigl(K[X,Y,Z]/(F,G)\bigr)_\ell
&\longrightarrow
\bigl(K[X,Y,Z]/(F,G)\bigr)_{\ell+\lambda},\\
H&\longmapsto Z^\lambda H
\end{aligned}
$$

is injective. Since both spaces have dimension $mn$, it is also bijective. With $\lambda=d+e-\ell$, the elements

$$
Z^\lambda V_i,\qquad i=1,\ldots,mn,
$$

form a basis of the component of degree $\ell+\lambda=d+e$.

> **Source correction AGC-CORR-0131 - the boundary case $\lambda=0$.** The source chooses $d+e\geq\ell$, but states the assertion about multiplication by $Z^\lambda$ only for $\lambda\geq1$. If $d+e=\ell$, then $\lambda=0$ and the required map is the identity; if $d+e>\ell$, the source's argument applies with $\lambda\geq1$. Distinguishing these cases makes the basis conclusion above valid for every choice with $d+e\geq\ell$.

Consequently there are $a_1,\ldots,a_{mn}\in K$ such that

$$
Z^eQ=\sum_{i=1}^{mn}a_iZ^{d+e-\ell}V_i.
$$

Dehomogenising this equation immediately expresses $q$ as a linear combination of $v_1,\ldots,v_{mn}$.

To prove linear independence, suppose

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{mn}a_iv_i=0
$$

in the quotient ring. Then in $K[X,Y]$ there is an equation

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{mn}a_iv_i
=\widetilde A\widetilde F+\widetilde B\widetilde G.
$$

Take homogeneous polynomials $A,B\in K[X,Y,Z]$ with dehomogenisations $\widetilde A,\widetilde B$, respectively. Thus we have two expressions with the same dehomogenisation: $\sum_i a_iV_i$, homogeneous of degree $\ell$, and $AF+BG$, a sum of two homogeneous polynomials whose degrees may differ.

By choosing suitable nonnegative integers $r,s,t$, we can make

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{mn}a_iZ^rV_i
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
Z^sAF+Z^tBG
$$

homogeneous of the same degree. By Exercise 6.9, equality of their dehomogenisations then implies

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{mn}a_iZ^rV_i=Z^sAF+Z^tBG.
$$

In $K[X,Y,Z]/(F,G)$, this equation means

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{mn}a_iZ^rV_i=0.
$$

Multiplication by $Z^r$ is injective: for $r=0$ it is the identity, whereas for $r>0$ this follows by repeated application of Lemma 30.2. Since $V_1,\ldots,V_{mn}$ form a basis, all $a_i=0$. Thus $v_1,\ldots,v_{mn}$ do indeed form a basis, so

$$
\dim_K\!\left(K[X,Y]/(\widetilde F,\widetilde G)\right)=mn.
$$

This proves the formula in Bézout's Theorem. $\square$

### Corollary 30.4: existence of an intersection point {#br-ak-2012-l30-cor-01}

Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field and let

$$
C,D\subseteq\mathbb P_K^2
$$

be projective plane curves. Then

$$
C\cap D\ne\varnothing.
$$

#### Proof {#br-ak-2012-l30-cor-01-proof}

The assertion is clearly true if $C$ and $D$ have a common component. Otherwise, it follows from Theorem 30.3. $\square$

<!-- upstream_entity: Schnitttheorie von Kurven/Satz von Bézout/Maximal mn Schnittpunkte/Fakt -->

### Corollary 30.5: at most $mn$ intersection points {#br-ak-2012-l30-cor-02}

Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field and let

$$
F,G\in K[X,Y,Z]
$$

be homogeneous polynomials of degrees $m$ and $n$ with no common component, with corresponding curves

$$
C=V_+(F),\qquad D=V_+(G)\subseteq\mathbb P_K^2.
$$

Then $C$ and $D$ have at most $mn$ intersection points.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2012-l30-cor-02-proof}

This follows directly from Theorem 30.3, since each intersection point contributes at least $1$ to the sum of intersection multiplicities. $\square$

## Example: the semicubical parabola and a circle {#br-ak-2012-l30-s04}

### Example 30.6: five points with total multiplicity six {#br-ak-2012-l30-ex-01}

In this example we work over $\mathbb C$. Consider the semicubical parabola

$$
C=V_+(ZY^2-X^3)
$$

and the circle with centre $(1,0,1)$,

$$
D=V_+\bigl((X-Z)^2+Y^2-Z^2\bigr).
$$

> **Source correction AGC-CORR-0132 - base field of the example.** The source does not specify a base field, but its calculation uses $\sqrt2\,\mathrm i$, and the displayed distinct-point description requires the absence of exceptional-characteristic issues. This edition sets $K=\mathbb C$, so the entire calculation, the five distinct points, and the application of Bézout's Theorem lie within a valid scope. The source equations are unchanged.

By Bézout's Theorem, we expect a total intersection multiplicity of $6$. Let us compute the intersection points. If $Z=0$, the first equation gives $X=0$, and the second then gives $Y=0$. This does not define a projective point, so there is no intersection point on the projective line $V_+(Z)$.

We therefore consider the affine equations

$$
Y^2-X^3=0,
\qquad
(X-1)^2+Y^2-1=0.
$$

Substituting

$$
Y^2=1-(X-1)^2
$$

into the first equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
1-(X-1)^2-X^3
&=-X^3-X^2+2X\\
&=X(-X^2-X+2)\\
&=-X(X-1)(X+2).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the intersection points are

$$
(0,0),\quad(1,1),\quad(1,-1),\quad
(-2,2\sqrt2\,\mathrm i),\quad
(-2,-2\sqrt2\,\mathrm i).
$$

The last two points also show why the algebraically closed field hypothesis is necessary: they would be absent if we worked only over $\mathbb R$. Hence there are five distinct intersection points. The semicubical parabola is singular at the origin, which is also an intersection point, so the intersection multiplicity there must exceed $1$. To confirm this, consider

$$
\begin{aligned}
&K[X,Y]_{(X,Y)}/\bigl(Y^2-X^3,Y^2-1+(X-1)^2\bigr)\\
&\quad=K[X,Y]_{(X,Y)}/\bigl(Y^2-X^3,X(X-1)(X+2)\bigr)\\
&\quad=K[X,Y]_{(X,Y)}/\bigl(Y^2-X^3,X\bigr)\\
&\quad=K[X,Y]_{(X,Y)}/\bigl(Y^2,X\bigr)\\
&\quad=K[Y]/(Y^2).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here we repeat the elimination above and then use the fact that $X-1$ and $X+2$ are units in the local ring $K[X,Y]_{(X,Y)}$. The dimension is $2$. Thus the intersection multiplicity at the origin is $2$, while it is $1$ at each of the other four points, as can also be checked directly. The sum is

$$
2+1+1+1+1=6=3\cdot2,
$$

exactly as asserted by Bézout's Theorem.
