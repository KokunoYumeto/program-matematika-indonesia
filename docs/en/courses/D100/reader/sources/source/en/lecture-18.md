# Lecture 18: Monomial Curves and Numerical Monoids {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18}

## Monomial curves {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-s01}

We now specialise the theory of monoid rings to the one-dimensional case,
obtaining the rings that describe monomial curves.

<!-- upstream_entity: Affine Kurven/Monomiale Kurve/Definition -->

### Definition: monomial curve {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-def-01}

A *monomial curve* is the image of the affine line $\mathbb A_K^1$ under
a map of the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb A_K^1&\longrightarrow\mathbb A_K^n,\\
t&\longmapsto(t^{e_1},\ldots,t^{e_n}),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $e_i\geq1$ for every $i$.

We shall shortly see that the image of such a monomial map is Zariski
closed. Thus a monomial curve really is an algebraic curve. A monomial
curve is parametrised and hence rational, although it is not generally a
plane curve. Sometimes the map itself is also called a monomial curve.

**Edition note:** the source's closed-image assertion holds for relatively
prime exponents over any field, and for arbitrary exponents over an
algebraically closed field. Without such a hypothesis it can fail:
$t\mapsto t^2$ over $\mathbb R$ has a non-Zariski-closed image.

We often restrict attention to exponents $e_i$ whose greatest common
divisor is $1$. This is no essential restriction. If $m$ is the greatest
common divisor of all the $e_i$, we can write

$$
e_i=mf_i
$$

with $f_1,\ldots,f_n$ relatively prime, and factor the map as

$$
\mathbb A_K^1\longrightarrow\mathbb A_K^1\longrightarrow\mathbb A_K^n,
\qquad
t\longmapsto t^m=s,
\qquad
s\longmapsto(s^{f_1},\ldots,s^{f_n}).
$$

The first map is simply exponentiation, while the second is a monomial
curve map with relatively prime exponents.

**Edition note:** although the domain of the second map is the affine line
with a single coordinate $s$, the source prints the image coordinates as
$(s_1^{f_1},\ldots,s_n^{f_n})$. This edition displays powers of the defined
scalar input, namely $(s^{f_1},\ldots,s^{f_n})$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Monomiale Kurvenabbildung/Monoidhomomorphismus/Bemerkung -->

### Remark: the underlying monoid map {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-rem-01}

The monomial map

$$
t\longmapsto(t^{e_1},\ldots,t^{e_n})
$$

is precisely the map of $K$-spectra associated with the monoid homomorphism

$$
\mathbb N^n\longrightarrow\mathbb N
$$

that sends the $i$th basis vector to $e_i$; compare Remark 17.9.

**Edition note:** the source prints the coordinates as
$(t_1^{e_1},\ldots,t_n^{e_n})$, although the input is a single scalar $t$.
This edition displays coordinates of the correct type.

This monoid homomorphism factors as

$$
\mathbb N^n\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow\mathbb N,
$$

where $M$ is the submonoid of $\mathbb N$ generated by $e_1,\ldots,e_n$.
Such a submonoid is called a *numerical monoid*. The first map is
surjective. At the ring level we obtain

$$
K[\mathbb N^n]=K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]
\longrightarrow K[M]
\longrightarrow K[\mathbb N]=K[T],
$$

while geometrically we obtain the spectrum maps

$$
\mathbb A_K^1
\longrightarrow
K\!-\!\operatorname{Spek}(K[M])
\subseteq\mathbb A_K^n.
$$

Thus the image of the affine line lies in the $K$-spectrum of the monoid
ring $K[M]$. Theorem 18.10 will show that

$$
\mathbb A_K^1\longrightarrow K\!-\!\operatorname{Spek}(K[M])
$$

is always surjective and, when the exponents are relatively prime, also
injective.

**Edition note:** “always surjective” requires, for example, algebraic
closure of $K$. For a general field, Theorem 18.10 proves bijectivity
under the relatively prime hypothesis. If $M=2\mathbb N$ and
$K=\mathbb R$, this spectrum map is $t\mapsto t^2$ and is not surjective.

### Example: Neil's parabola {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-exa-01}

![A cuspidal curve with two smooth arcs meeting in a sharp point at the origin](authority/assets/Cusp-500.png)

*Neil's parabola with a cuspidal singularity at the origin. Georg-Johann,
[CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/). Source
details and the discrepancy between rights labels are recorded in the
Unit 18 media credits.*

*Neil's parabola* $C$ is the image of the monomial map

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb A_K^1&\longrightarrow\mathbb A_K^2,\\
t&\longmapsto(t^2,t^3)=(x,y).
\end{aligned}
$$

The corresponding equation is

$$
y^2=x^3,
$$

so that

$$
C=V(Y^2-X^3).
$$

A monomial curve is determined solely by its tuple of exponents
$(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$, or equivalently by the numerical monoid they generate.
Thus very little combinatorial information is required. Nevertheless,
these curves provide a rich collection of examples in the theory of
algebraic curves. The same phenomenon holds more generally for monoid
rings and the algebraic varieties they define.

## Invariants of numerical monoids {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-s02}

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Halbgruppe/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Ab n alles/Fakt -->

### Lemma: all sufficiently large numbers belong to the monoid {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-lem-01}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a numerical monoid generated by relatively
prime natural numbers $e_1,\ldots,e_n$. Every $m\in\mathbb N$ has a
representation

$$
m=a_1e_1+\cdots+a_ne_n
$$

with

$$
0\leq a_i<e_{i+1},
\qquad 1\leq i\leq n-1.
$$

If $m$ is sufficiently large, we can also arrange that $a_n\geq0$, so that
all coefficients in the representation are nonnegative.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-lem-01-proof}

Since $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ are relatively prime, there is initially a
representation

$$
m=b_1e_1+\cdots+b_ne_n
$$

with integer coefficients. We put it into the required form step by step.
By division with remainder, write

$$
b_1=c_1e_2+a_1,
\qquad
0\leq a_1<e_2.
$$

Substitute this into the representation of $m$ and combine the term
$c_1e_2e_1$ with $b_2e_2$. Treat the new second coefficient in the same way,
using the third generator. Continuing this process puts the first $n-1$
coefficients into the required form.

In this form, the sum of the first $n-1$ terms is bounded by a constant
depending only on the generators. If $m$ exceeds this bound, the last term,
and therefore its coefficient $a_n$, must be nonnegative.

Thus, beyond some threshold, every natural number belongs to the monoid
generated by the relatively prime exponents. The least such threshold
has its own name.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Halbgruppe/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Führungszahl/Definition -->

### Definition: conductor {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-def-02}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a numerical monoid generated by relatively
prime elements. The least number $f$ satisfying

$$
\mathbb N_{\geq f}\subseteq M
$$

is called the *conductor* of $M$.

The following invariants can also be computed discretely at the level of
the numerical monoid. Later we shall define them for arbitrary algebraic
curves; in that generality, they are usually harder to compute.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Halbgruppe/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Numerische Einbettungsdimension/Definition -->

### Definition: embedding dimension {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-def-03}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a numerical monoid with relatively prime
generators. The smallest number of elements in a generating system for
$M$ is called its *embedding dimension*.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Monoide/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Numerische Multiplizität/Definition -->

### Definition: multiplicity {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-def-04}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a numerical monoid with relatively prime
generators. The least positive element

$$
e\in M,
\qquad e\geq1,
$$

is called the *multiplicity* of $M$ and is denoted by $e(M)$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Halbgruppe/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Singularitätsgrad/Definition -->

### Definition: degree of singularity {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-def-05}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a numerical monoid with relatively prime
generators. The number of gaps, that is, the number of elements of

$$
\mathbb N\setminus M,
$$

is called the *degree of singularity* of $M$ and is denoted by $\delta(M)$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerisches Monoid/5,8,11/Invarianten/Beispiel -->

### Example: the monoid generated by 5, 8, and 11 {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-exa-02}

Consider the numerical monoid $M$ generated by $5,8,11$. Thus $M$ consists
of all sums

$$
5a_1+8a_2+11a_3,
\qquad a_1,a_2,a_3\geq0.
$$

Its elements include

$$
0,5,8,10,11,13,15,16,18,19,20,21,22,23,24,25,\ldots.
$$

Since $18,19,20,21,22$ are five consecutive numbers in $M$ and $5\in M$,
all subsequent numbers also belong to $M$. Hence its conductor is $18$,
its multiplicity is $5$, its degree of singularity is $10$, and its
embedding dimension is $3$.

## Parametrisation and canonical generators {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-s03}

<!-- upstream_entity: Monomiale Kurvenabbildung/Bijektiv/Fakt -->

### Theorem: the monomial parametrisation is a bijection {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-thm-01}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a submonoid generated by relatively prime
natural numbers $e_1,\ldots,e_n$. The monomial map

$$
\mathbb A_K^1\longrightarrow K\!-\!\operatorname{Spek}(K[M])
$$

is a bijection.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-thm-01-proof}

By Remark 17.9, this can be viewed as the natural map

$$
\mathbb A_K^1
=\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb N,K)
\longrightarrow
\operatorname{Mor}_{\mathrm{mon}}(M,K)
$$

induced by the inclusion $M\subseteq\mathbb N$.

To prove injectivity, suppose that $a,b\in K$ and

$$
a^m=b^m
$$

for every $m\in M$. If $b=0$, then immediately $a=0$. We may therefore
assume $b\ne0$, and likewise $a\ne0$. By Lemma 18.4, all natural numbers
from some $f$ onwards belong to $M$. In particular,

$$
a^f=b^f,
\qquad
a^{f+1}=b^{f+1}.
$$

It follows that

$$
a=\frac{a^{f+1}}{a^f}
=\frac{b^{f+1}}{b^f}
=b.
$$

For surjectivity, take a monoid homomorphism

$$
\varphi:M\longrightarrow K.
$$

We must extend it to all of $\mathbb N$. Write

$$
\varphi(e_i)=a_i\in K.
$$

These values satisfy

$$
a_j^{e_i}
=\varphi(e_j)^{e_i}
=\varphi(e_ie_j)
=\varphi(e_i)^{e_j}
=a_i^{e_j}.
$$

If one $a_i$ is $0$, then all the $a_i$ are $0$, and the map sending every
positive number to $0$ (and $0$ to $1$) gives an extension. Thus we may
assume that all the $a_i$ are units.

Since the $e_i$ are relatively prime, there are integers
$m_1,\ldots,m_n\in\mathbb Z$ such that

$$
m_1e_1+\cdots+m_ne_n=1.
$$

Set

$$
a=a_1^{m_1}\cdots a_n^{m_n}.
$$

We claim that the homomorphism $\mathbb N\to K$ determined by
$1\mapsto a$, namely $k\mapsto a^k$, extends $\varphi$. It suffices to
check this on the $e_i$. For $e_1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^{e_1}
&=(a_1^{m_1}\cdots a_n^{m_n})^{e_1}\\
&=a_1^{e_1m_1}a_2^{e_1m_2}\cdots a_n^{e_1m_n}\\
&=a_1^{1-\sum_{i=2}^nm_ie_i}
  (a_2^{e_1m_2}\cdots a_n^{e_1m_n})\\
&=a_1(a_1^{-m_2e_2}a_2^{e_1m_2})
  \cdots(a_1^{-m_ne_n}a_n^{e_1m_n})\\
&=a_1(a_1^{-e_2}a_2^{e_1})^{m_2}
  \cdots(a_1^{-e_n}a_n^{e_1})^{m_n}\\
&=a_1,
\end{aligned}
$$

because each factor in the penultimate line equals $1$ by the relations
$a_j^{e_i}=a_i^{e_j}$. The same argument applies to every $e_i$.

### Remark: proof using the group of differences {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-rem-02}

The surjectivity in the preceding theorem can also be seen using the
universal property of the group of differences. The group of differences
of a numerical monoid with relatively prime generators is $\mathbb Z$.
The case in which a generator maps to zero is handled as in the proof.
If all images are nonzero, we obtain a monoid homomorphism

$$
\varphi:M\longrightarrow K^\times.
$$

The universal property of the group of differences gives a unique extension

$$
\widetilde\varphi:\mathbb Z\longrightarrow K^\times,
$$

which supplies the required preimage.

**Edition note:** at this step the source refers to the universal property
of monoid rings. The construction actually used extends from $M$ to its
group of differences $\mathbb Z$, so this edition states the universal
property of the group of differences.

The next two statements show that a numerical monoid has a canonical
generating system. This gives the embedding dimension an interpretation
that can later be transferred to arbitrary Noetherian local rings. The
monoid ring itself is of course not local, but its localisation at the
singularity of a numerical monoid is local.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Halbgruppe/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Minimales Standard-Erzeugendensystem/Fakt -->

### Lemma: the canonical minimal generating system {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-lem-02}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a numerical monoid with relatively prime
generators. Set

$$
M_+=\{m\in M\mid m\geq1\}
$$

and

$$
M_++M_+=\{m+m'\mid m,m'\in M_+\}.
$$

Then

$$
M_+\setminus(M_++M_+)
$$

is a generating system for $M$, and every other generating system contains
this set.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-lem-02-proof}

An element

$$
m\in M_+\setminus(M_++M_+)
$$

cannot be written as a sum of two other positive elements of $M$. It
therefore belongs to every generating system.

Conversely, this set already generates $M$. Otherwise, choose the least
element $x\in M$ that it does not generate. Since $x$ does not belong to
$M_+\setminus(M_++M_+)$, we have

$$
x=x_1+x_2,
\qquad x_1,x_2\in M_+.
$$

Both summands are smaller than $x$, so each can be written as a sum of
elements of the canonical set. This immediately gives a contradiction.

**Edition note:** the source prints $x_1,x_2\in M_++M_+$. From
$x\in M_++M_+$, the definition of the sumset gives only
$x_1,x_2\in M_+$; this is the membership needed for the minimal-descent
argument.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerische Halbgruppe/Teilerfremde Erzeuger/Numerische Einbettungsdimension/Charakterisierung mit M+ ohne M+^2/Fakt -->

### Corollary: embedding dimension from the canonical generators {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-cor-01}

In the situation of the preceding lemma, the embedding dimension of $M$
equals the number of elements of

$$
M_+\setminus(M_++M_+).
$$

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l18-cor-01-proof}

This follows directly from Lemma 18.12.
