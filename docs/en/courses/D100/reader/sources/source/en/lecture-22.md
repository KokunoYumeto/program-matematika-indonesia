# Lecture 22: Embedding Dimension and Smooth and Singular Points {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22}

## Embedding dimension {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-s01}

<!-- upstream_entity: Lokale kommutative noethersche Ringe/Minimale Erzeugendenzahl des maximalen Ideals/Definition -->

### Definition: embedding dimension {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-def-01}

Let $R$ be a Noetherian local commutative ring with maximal ideal
$\mathfrak m$. The minimum number of generators of the ideal $\mathfrak m$
is called the *embedding dimension* of $R$, written

$$
\operatorname{embdim}(R).
$$

<!-- upstream_entity: Einbettungsdimension/Numerische Situation/Einführung/Textabschnitt -->

A Noetherian local integral domain of dimension one—that is, whose only
prime ideals are the zero ideal and the maximal ideal—is a discrete valuation
ring if and only if its embedding dimension is $1$, by Theorem 21.8. The
embedding dimension is always at least as large as the dimension of a local
ring. Rings for which equality holds play a special role and are called
*regular rings*. We have already encountered embedding dimension for
monomial curves and must show that the definition given there (Definition
18.6) agrees with this new one.

We first prove another characterisation, which follows from Nakayama's
lemma.

<!-- upstream_entity: Lokaler Ring/Modulerzeuger und Erzeuger mod m/Fakt -->

### Lemma: module generators and generators modulo $\mathfrak m$ {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-01}

Let $(R,\mathfrak m,K)$ be a local ring and $V$ a finitely generated
$R$-module. Then the minimum number of generators

$$
\mu(V)
$$

equals the dimension of the $K$-vector space

$$
V/\mathfrak mV.
$$

<!-- upstream_entity: Lokaler Ring/Modulerzeuger und Erzeuger mod m/Fakt/Beweis -->

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-01-proof}

We prove the slightly more general statement that elements

$$
v_1,\ldots,v_n\in V
$$

generate $V$ as an $R$-module if and only if their residue classes in
$V/\mathfrak mV$ generate it over $R/\mathfrak m$. One direction is
immediate. Thus let $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in V$ be elements whose classes modulo
$\mathfrak m$ form a generating set. Let

$$
U\subseteq V
$$

be the $R$-submodule generated by the $v_i$. The hypothesis means that

$$
V=U+\mathfrak mV.
$$

Consider the quotient module $V/U$. In it we have

$$
(V/U)\mathfrak m=V/U.
$$

Nakayama's lemma gives

$$
V/U=0,
$$

and hence

$$
V=U.
$$

<!-- upstream_entity: Lokaler Ring/Einbettungsdimension ist Dimension des Kotangentialraumes/Fakt -->

### Corollary: embedding dimension as the dimension of the cotangent space {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-cor-01}

Let $(R,\mathfrak m,K)$ be a Noetherian local ring. Its embedding dimension
equals

$$
\mu(\mathfrak m)
=
\dim_K\left(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\right).
$$

<!-- upstream_entity: Lokaler Ring/Einbettungsdimension ist Dimension des Kotangentialraumes/Fakt/Beweis -->

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-cor-01-proof}

This follows immediately from Nakayama's lemma applied to the ideal
$\mathfrak m$ and the finitely generated $R$-module $\mathfrak m$.

The $R$-module

$$
\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2,
$$

which occurs above and is a vector space over $R/\mathfrak m$, is also
called the *cotangent space* of the local ring.

<!-- upstream_entity: Noetherscher Ring/Maximales Ideal/Kotangentialraum direkt und über lokalen Ring/Fakt -->

### Lemma: the cotangent space before and after localisation {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-02}

Let $R$ be a Noetherian commutative ring and $\mathfrak n$ a maximal ideal.
Let

$$
S=R_{\mathfrak n}
$$

be the localisation at $\mathfrak n$, with maximal ideal

$$
\mathfrak m=\mathfrak nR_{\mathfrak n}.
$$

Then

$$
\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2
\cong
\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2.
$$

In particular, the embedding dimension of the localisation equals

$$
\dim_{R/\mathfrak n}\left(\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2\right).
$$

<!-- upstream_entity: Noetherscher Ring/Maximales Ideal/Kotangentialraum direkt und über lokalen Ring/Fakt/Beweis -->

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-02-proof}

By Exercise 15.5,

$$
R/\mathfrak n
\cong
R_{\mathfrak n}/\mathfrak m,
$$

so the residue fields agree; denote this common residue field by $K$. The
natural $R$-module homomorphism

$$
\mathfrak n\longrightarrow\mathfrak m
$$

induces a homomorphism of $K$-vector spaces

$$
\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2
\longrightarrow
\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2.
$$

This map is surjective, since $R$-module generators of $\mathfrak n$ map to
$R_{\mathfrak n}$-module generators of $\mathfrak m$, whose classes modulo
$\mathfrak m^2$ generate the $K$-vector space.

To prove injectivity, take

$$
f\in\mathfrak n
$$

that maps to $0$ on the right. This means that

$$
f\in\mathfrak m^2
$$

in the localisation $R_{\mathfrak n}$. There are therefore elements

$$
g_1,\ldots,g_n\in\mathfrak n,
\qquad
h_1,\ldots,h_n\in\mathfrak n,
$$

and elements

$$
q_i=\frac{a_i}{s_i}\in R_{\mathfrak n},
\qquad
s_i\notin\mathfrak n,
\qquad
a_i\in R,
$$

such that

$$
f=\frac{a_1}{s_1}g_1h_1+\cdots+\frac{a_n}{s_n}g_nh_n.
$$

Translated back into $R$, this means that there is an element

$$
s\notin\mathfrak n
$$

with

$$
sf=b_1g_1h_1+\cdots+b_ng_nh_n
$$

for some $b_i\in R$. Since $s$ does not belong to the maximal ideal
$\mathfrak n$, there are $r\in R$ and $g\in\mathfrak n$ such that

$$
g+rs=1.
$$

Multiplying the preceding equation by $r$, we obtain

$$
(1-g)f
=
r\left(b_1g_1h_1+\cdots+b_ng_nh_n\right),
$$

or, equivalently,

$$
f
=
r\left(b_1g_1h_1+\cdots+b_ng_nh_n\right)+gf.
$$

The right-hand side plainly belongs to $\mathfrak n^2$. Thus $f$ represents
zero in $\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2$, proving injectivity.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerisches Monoid/Lokale kommutative noethersche Ringe/Numerische und algebraische Einbettungsdimension/Äquivalenz/Fakt -->

### Lemma: equality of numerical and algebraic embedding dimension {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-03}

Let $K$ be a field and $M$ a numerical monoid generated by coprime natural
numbers. Let

$$
R=K[M]
$$

be the corresponding monoid ring, with maximal ideal

$$
\mathfrak n=(M_+),
$$

and let $R_{\mathfrak n}$ be its localisation. Then the numerical embedding
dimension of $M$—or of $K[M]$—equals the embedding dimension of the local
ring $R_{\mathfrak n}$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Numerisches Monoid/Lokale kommutative noethersche Ringe/Numerische und algebraische Einbettungsdimension/Äquivalenz/Fakt/Beweis -->

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-03-proof}

We have

$$
\mathfrak n
=(M_+)
=\bigoplus_{m\in M_+}K\,T^m
$$

and

$$
\mathfrak n^2
=(2M_+)
=\bigoplus_{m\in2M_+}K\,T^m.
$$

The quotient space is therefore

$$
\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2
=
\bigoplus_{m\in M_+\setminus2M_+}K\,T^m.
$$

Its $K$-dimension equals the number of elements of
$M_+\setminus2M_+$. By Corollary 18.13, $M_+\setminus2M_+$ is the minimal
monoid generating set of $M$. Thus this $K$-dimension equals the numerical
embedding dimension.

On the other hand, by Lemma 22.4, the $K$-dimension of
$\mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2$ equals the embedding dimension of the
corresponding local ring $R_{\mathfrak n}$.

## Smooth and singular points {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-s02}

<!-- upstream_entity: Algebraische ebene Kurve/Multiplizität/Glatte und singuläre Punkte/Partielle Ableitungen/Kartesisches Blatt/Einführung/Textabschnitt -->

Let $K$ be a field and

$$
F\in K[X,Y],
\qquad
F\ne0,
$$

a polynomial without repeated factors. Since we are interested only in the
corresponding curve, over an algebraically closed field this is no restriction,
by Hilbert's Nullstellensatz. For every point

$$
(a,b)\in\mathbb A_K^2,
$$

we may pass to the variables $X-a$ and $Y-b$. This shifts the point to the
origin. Thus, when studying a polynomial's behaviour at a point, we may
always restrict attention to the origin.

Now let

$$
P=(0,0).
$$

Write $F$ in terms of its homogeneous components as

$$
F=F_d+F_{d-1}+\cdots+F_1+F_0,
$$

where $F_i$ is homogeneous of degree $i$. What can we read off from the
individual homogeneous components? First, we immediately have

$$
P\in V(F)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
F_0=0.
$$

Substituting the coordinates of $P$, namely $(0,0)$, into $F$ makes all the
higher-degree components vanish, leaving only the constant component $F_0$.
Since our main interest is the behaviour of a curve at one of its points,
we shall often restrict attention to the situation

$$
F_0=0.
$$

Which is the first nonzero homogeneous component $F_i$? What role does this
index $i$ play, and what role do its linear factors play?

Suppose first that

$$
F_0=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
F_1=aX+bY.
$$

This linear form, which may be zero, can also be characterised by partial
derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P)=a
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)=b.
$$

Here and below, polynomials are differentiated formally. Thus

$$
F_1=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P)
=
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)
=0.
$$

If this does not hold, it is natural to regard the line defined by

$$
F_1(X,Y)=0
$$

as the tangent to the curve at $P$. An initial indication is that in the
linear case

$$
F=F_1,
$$

the line should coincide with its tangent.

<!-- upstream_entity: Ebene algebraische Kurven/Punkt/Glatt mit partiellen Ableitungen/Definition -->

### Definition: smooth and singular points {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-def-02}

Let $K$ be a field and $F\in K[X,Y]$ a nonzero polynomial. Let

$$
P\in C=V(F)\subseteq\mathbb A_K^2
$$

be a point on the corresponding affine plane curve. The point $P$ is called
a *smooth point* of $C$ if

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P)\ne0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)\ne0.
$$

Otherwise it is called *singular*.

The curve is called *smooth* if it is smooth at every one of its points.

> **Edition bridge 22.A — scope of the terminology.** This lecture works
> with plane curves presented by a polynomial $F$ without repeated factors,
> as stipulated above. In this definition, “smooth” means the formal
> derivative criterion just stated for that presentation. The edition does
> not extend this to an unconditional identification with scheme-theoretic
> smoothness over arbitrary fields, particularly imperfect fields.

### Definition: multiplicity and tangent lines {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-def-03}

Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field and $F\in K[X,Y]$ a nonzero
polynomial. Let

$$
P\in C=V(F)\subseteq\mathbb A_K^2
$$

be a point on the corresponding affine plane curve, and suppose that $P$ is
the origin after an affine change of coordinates by translation. Let

$$
F=F_d+F_{d-1}+\cdots+F_m
$$

be the homogeneous decomposition of $F$, with

$$
F_d\ne0,
\qquad
F_m\ne0,
\qquad
d\geq m.
$$

The number $m$ is called the *multiplicity* of the curve at $P$. Let

$$
F_m=G_1\cdots G_m
$$

be its decomposition into linear factors. Each line

$$
V(G_i),
\qquad i=1,\ldots,m,
$$

is called a *tangent line* to $C$ at $P$. The multiplicity of $G_i$ in $F_m$
is also called the *multiplicity* of that tangent line.

**Edition note — source correction.** The source calls moving a general
point $P$ to the origin a “linear transformation of variables”. Translation
$(X,Y)\mapsto(X-a,Y-b)$ is an affine change of coordinates, not a linear
transformation unless $P=(0,0)$. The edition uses the correct term without
changing the homogeneous decomposition in the new coordinates.

The point is smooth if and only if its multiplicity is $1$. In that case
there is exactly one tangent line through the point, and its slope can be
computed from the partial derivatives.

### Example: several lines through the origin {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-exa-01}

Suppose that $d$ distinct lines

$$
L_1,\ldots,L_d
$$

in the affine plane are given, all passing through the origin. Let

$$
a_iX+b_iY=0,
\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,
$$

be their equations, which are determined only up to a scalar. Their union
is described by the product

$$
F=(a_1X+b_1Y)\cdots(a_dX+b_dY).
$$

In particular,

$$
F=F_d
$$

is homogeneous of degree $d$. Here each linear factor defines a tangent
line through the origin. The multiplicity is $d$.

### Example: the folium of Descartes {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-exa-02}

![Portrait of René Descartes with shoulder-length hair, a white collar, and black clothing against a dark background](authority/assets/250px-Frans_Hals_-_Portret_van_René_Descartes.jpg)

*René Descartes (1596–1650). Portrait after Frans Hals; uploaded to Commons
by Dedden; public domain; local file:
`authority/assets/250px-Frans_Hals_-_Portret_van_René_Descartes.jpg`.*

![Green graph of the folium of Descartes with a loop in the first quadrant, a node at the origin, and a dashed blue asymptote](authority/assets/Kartesisches-Blatt.svg)

*The folium of Descartes $x^3+y^3-3xy=0$, with axes, grid, and asymptote.
Georg-Johann, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/);
local file: `authority/assets/Kartesisches-Blatt.svg`.*

**Edition note — characteristic.** The assumption
$\operatorname{char}(K)\ne3$ applies throughout this example. The source
states it below when discussing the other points, but it is already needed
for the multiplicity-two assertion at the origin: in characteristic $3$,
$F_2=0$ and $F=(X+Y)^3$, contrary to the standing squarefree premise.

The *folium of Descartes* is described by the equation

$$
F=X^3+Y^3-3XY=0.
$$

The number $3$ is not essential here and can be replaced by any other
nonzero number. The homogeneous components of the curve equation are

$$
F_3=X^3+Y^3
$$

and

$$
F_2=-3XY.
$$

Thus the origin on the folium of Descartes has multiplicity two and is
singular. Both the $X$-axis and the $Y$-axis are tangent lines, each with
multiplicity one. At every other point the curve is smooth, provided the
base field does not have characteristic $3$. From

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}=3X^2-3Y=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}=3Y^2-3X=0
$$

we obtain

$$
Y=X^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
X=Y^2,
$$

and hence also

$$
Y=Y^4,
$$

and similarly for $X$. Thus

$$
Y=X=0,
$$

or $X$ and $Y$ are both cube roots of unity: either both are $1$, or they
are the other two cube roots of unity, in either order. However, at these
other common zeros of the partial derivatives, $F$ takes the value $-1$.
These are therefore not points of the curve.

### Remark: the tangent-line equation at a smooth point {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-rem-01}

At a smooth point

$$
P\in C=V(F)
$$

of a plane algebraic curve, the multiplicity is

$$
m=1.
$$

If $P=(0,0)$, the linear term of the curve equation is

$$
F_1=uX+vY=0,
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P)=u
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)=v,
$$

since the higher-degree homogeneous components of $F$ contribute nothing
to the partial derivatives at the origin. Thus this linear equation is
the tangent-line equation.

For an arbitrary smooth point

$$
P=(a,b)\in C,
$$

the tangent-line equation can likewise be read off directly from the
partial derivatives of $F$ at $P$. The tangent line is given by

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P)(X-a)
+
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)(Y-b)
=0.
$$

<!-- upstream_entity: Ebene algebraische Kurven/Tangentialabbildung und Tangente in einem glatten Punkt als Kern/Bemerkung -->

### Remark: the tangent map and the direction of the tangent line {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-rem-02}

Let $F\in K[X,Y]$ have corresponding plane algebraic curve $C$, and let

$$
P\in C=V(F)
$$

be a smooth point of the curve. The map

$$
F:\mathbb A_K^2\longrightarrow\mathbb A_K^1
$$

and the point $P$ determine a linear *tangent map*, also called the *total
differential*, between the corresponding tangent spaces:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_PF
=
\left(
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P),
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)
\right):
T_P\mathbb A_K^2\cong\mathbb A_K^2
&\longrightarrow
T_{F(P)}\mathbb A_K^1
=T_0\mathbb A_K^1
\cong\mathbb A_K^1,\\
(s,t)
&\longmapsto
\frac{\partial F}{\partial X}(P)s
+
\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(P)t.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $P$ is smooth, this linear map is not the zero map. The direction of
the tangent line to $C$ at $P$ is the kernel of this tangent map. When
identifying the tangent plane at $P$ with the surrounding affine plane,
we must identify $P$ with the origin: the tangent line must pass through
that point, whereas the kernel specifies only a linear direction.

The following statement shows that an intersection point of two irreducible
components can never be smooth.

### Lemma: a smooth point lies on only one component {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-lem-04}

Let

$$
C=V(F)
$$

be a plane algebraic curve and

$$
F=F_1\cdots F_n
$$

its decomposition into distinct prime factors. Let $P\in C$ be a smooth
point of the curve. Then $P$ lies on only one component

$$
C_i=V(F_i)
$$

of the curve.

**Proof.** See Exercise 22.9.

<!-- upstream_entity: Ebene algebraische Kurve/Glatte zusammenhängende Kurve/Ist irreduzibel/Fakt -->

### Corollary: a smooth connected curve is irreducible {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-cor-02}

Let

$$
C\subseteq\mathbb A_K^2
$$

be a smooth plane algebraic curve, connected in the Zariski topology, over
an algebraically closed field $K$. Then $C$ is irreducible.

<!-- upstream_entity: Ebene algebraische Kurve/Glatte zusammenhängende Kurve/Ist irreduzibel/Fakt/Beweis -->

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l22-cor-02-proof}

By Lemma 22.12, the irreducible components of the curve are disjoint. They
are then also its connected components. Thus there is only one irreducible
component, so the curve is irreducible.
