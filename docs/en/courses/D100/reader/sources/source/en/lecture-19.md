# Lecture 19: Quotient Ring Presentations and Integrality {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19}

## Quotient ring presentations for monomial curves {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-s01}

Let

$$
M\subseteq\mathbb N
$$

be a numerical monoid generated by natural numbers $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ with $\gcd(e_1,\ldots,e_n)=1$. The associated surjection

$$
\mathbb N^n\longrightarrow M\subseteq\mathbb N
$$

gives a surjection

$$
\begin{aligned}
K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]&\longrightarrow K[M],\\
X_i&\longmapsto T^{e_i},
\end{aligned}
$$

and a closed embedding

$$
C=K\!-\!\operatorname{Spek}(K[M])\hookrightarrow\mathbb A_K^n.
$$

Which equations describe the curve $C$?

<!-- upstream_entity: Affine Kurven/Monomiale Kurven/Beschreibende binomiale Gleichungen/Fakt -->

### Theorem: binomial equations defining the curve {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-thm-01}

Let $M\subseteq\mathbb N$ be a submonoid generated by $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ with $\gcd(e_1,\ldots,e_n)=1$, and let

$$
\mathbb N^n\longrightarrow M
$$

be the associated surjective map, with quotient homomorphism

$$
\varphi:K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]\longrightarrow K[M].
$$

Then the kernel ideal is described by

$$
\ker\varphi=
\left(
\prod_{i\in I_1}X_i^{r_i}-\prod_{i\in I_2}X_i^{s_i}
\mathrel{\Bigg|}
\begin{array}{l}
I_1,I_2\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\text{ disjoint},\\
\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_1}r_ie_i=
\sum_{i\in I_2}s_ie_i,\\
r_i,s_i\geq1
\end{array}
\right).
$$

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-thm-01-proof}

The displayed elements belong to the kernel ideal, as follows directly from

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi\left(\prod_{i\in I_1}X_i^{r_i}\right)
&=\prod_{i\in I_1}\left(T^{e_i}\right)^{r_i}\\
&=T^{\sum_{i\in I_1}r_ie_i}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Edition note:** in this line the source switches from the variable $T$ specified in the homomorphism to lowercase $t$. This edition consistently uses the single variable $T$; no mathematical content is changed.

Conversely, let

$$
F\in K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]
$$

be a polynomial with $\varphi(F)=0$. Write

$$
F=\sum_\nu a_\nu X^\nu,
\qquad
\nu=(\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_n).
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(F)
&=\sum_\nu a_\nu T^{\sum_{i=1}^n\nu_ie_i}\\
&=\sum_{k\geq0}
\left(
\sum_{\nu:\,\sum_{i=1}^n\nu_ie_i=k}a_\nu
\right)T^k.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Edition note:** the source writes the summation bound `\sum_{k=0}` without an upper bound. Since $F$ is a polynomial and this line groups terms by nonnegative weight $k$, this edition writes $\sum_{k\geq0}$; only the notation is completed.

Since this polynomial is zero, all its coefficients vanish. Hence, for every $k$, the polynomial

$$
F_k=
\sum_{\nu:\,\sum_{i=1}^n\nu_ie_i=k}a_\nu X^\nu
$$

also lies in the kernel. We may therefore assume that $F$ contains only monomials $X^\nu$ with the same value

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n\nu_ie_i=k.
$$

Take a monomial $X^\nu$ appearing in $F$ with $a_\nu\ne0$. At least one other monomial, say $X^\mu$, must also appear, since a single monomial does not map to zero. Write

$$
F=a_\nu(X^\nu-X^\mu)
+\left(F-a_\nu X^\nu+a_\nu X^\mu\right).
$$

The monomial $X^\nu$ no longer appears in the summand on the right, and no new monomial is introduced. In $X^\nu-X^\mu$, factor out the variables occurring on both sides as far as possible. This gives

$$
X^\nu-X^\mu
=X_1^{b_1}\cdots X_n^{b_n}
\left(
\prod_{i\in I_1}X_i^{r_i}
-\prod_{i\in I_2}X_i^{s_i}
\right),
$$

with $I_1$ and $I_2$ disjoint and

$$
\sum_{i\in I_1}e_ir_i=\sum_{i\in I_2}e_is_i.
$$

Thus the first summand in the above expression for $F$ belongs to the ideal generated by the stated binomials. We can continue with the second summand, which contains one fewer monomial. This process terminates and proves the claim.

The equations in the preceding theorem are called *binomial equations*. The simplest binomial equations have the form

$$
X_i^{e_j/\gcd(e_i,e_j)}
=X_j^{e_i/\gcd(e_i,e_j)},
\qquad i\ne j.
$$

For a plane monomial curve, this is also the only equation.

### Corollary: the equation of a plane monomial curve {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-cor-01}

Let $C$ be the plane monomial curve given by

$$
t\longmapsto(t^{e_1},t^{e_2})=(x,y),
$$

with $e_1$ and $e_2$ relatively prime. Then

$$
C=V(X^{e_2}-Y^{e_1}).
$$

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-cor-01-proof}

This follows immediately from Theorem 19.1.

For monomial space curves, the defining equations can still be determined relatively easily, since one can always isolate a single variable.

### Example: the twisted cubic {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-exa-01}

![A green space curve twisting along two translucent surfaces inside a three-dimensional box](authority/assets/Twisted_cubic_curve.png)

*The twisted cubic. Claudio Rocchini,

has an inline CC BY-SA 3.0 label, whereas the frozen Commons metadata
offers the CC BY 3.0 option used for this component.*

Let

$$
C\subset\mathbb A_K^3
$$

be the *twisted cubic*, that is, the image of the monomial map

$$
t\longmapsto(t,t^2,t^3).
$$

This curve is isomorphic to the affine line and is therefore smooth. By Theorem 19.1, its defining ideal is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathfrak a
&=(Y-X^2,Z-X^3,Y^3-Z^2,Z-XY)\\
&=(Y-X^2,Z-X^3).
\end{aligned}
$$

The last two ideal generators are redundant, since they can be expressed in terms of the first two. Thus

$$
C=V(Y-X^2,Z-X^3).
$$

The images of $C$ under the three different projections are

$$
C_1=V(Z^2-Y^3),\qquad
C_2=V(Z-X^3),\qquad
C_3=V(Y-X^2).
$$

The curves $C_2$ and $C_3$ are isomorphic to the affine line, being graphs of maps, whereas $C_1$ is the singular Neil's parabola.

### Example: the monomial curve with exponents 3, 4, and 5 {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-exa-02}

Let $C$ be the monomial curve given by

$$
t\longmapsto(t^3,t^4,t^5)=(x,y,z).
$$

For each of the three variables, Theorem 19.1 requires us to determine which powers, after substituting the powers of $t$, can also be expressed as monomials in the other two variables.

First, the equations involving only two variables are

$$
Y^3=X^4,\qquad Z^3=X^5,\qquad Z^4=Y^5.
$$

As in the plane case, there can be only one basic relation for each pair of variables.

In a relation involving all three variables, one variable occurs alone on one side. Start with $X$. Neither $X$ nor $X^2$ can be expressed using the other variables, but

$$
X^3=T^9=YZ.
$$

No other combination independent of this relation is possible. In general, a double representation

$$
X^k=Y^iZ^j=Y^aZ^b
$$

gives a relation between powers of $Y$ and powers of $Z$, after cancelling the smaller powers. Since all relations involving only two variables have already been listed, each power of $X$ gives at most one new relation.

We have already finished with relations in which $X$ stands alone. Indeed, if

$$
X^k=Y^iZ^j,
$$

then $k\geq3$. If $i=0$ or $j=0$, the relation is already listed. Thus assume $i,j\geq1$. Using $X^3=YZ$, we can reduce the exponents in the equation: subtract $3$ from the exponent of $X$ and $1$ from each exponent of $Y$ and $Z$.

For $Y$ we immediately obtain

$$
Y^2=ZX,
$$

which again reduces all other equations. For $Z$ we obtain

$$
Z^2=X^2Y
$$

and

$$
Z^3=XY^3.
$$

There are no smaller monomials in $X$ and $Y$ that can be expressed as a power of $Z$. Hence every other relation can be reduced to an earlier one using one of these relations.

Altogether, the curve $C$ has the presentation

$$
C=V(Y^3-X^4,\ Z^3-X^5,\ Z^4-Y^5,\ X^3-YZ,\ Y^2-XZ,\ Z^2-X^2Y,\ Z^3-XY^3).
$$

**Edition note:** in this final list the source invokes the unbraced fraction macro `\mathdisplaybruch` immediately before the relation $X^3-YZ$. This edition displays the relation stated and proved immediately beforehand, $X^3-YZ$.

## Integrality {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-s02}

<!-- upstream_entity: Kommutative Ringtheorie/Ganzheit/Ganzheitsgleichung/Definition -->

### Definition: equation of integral dependence {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-def-01}

Let $R$ and $S$ be commutative rings and

$$
R\subseteq S
$$

a ring extension. For $x\in S$, an equation of the form

$$
x^n+r_{n-1}x^{n-1}+r_{n-2}x^{n-2}+\cdots+r_1x+r_0=0,
$$

with $r_i\in R$ for $i=0,\ldots,n-1$ is called an *equation of integral dependence* for $x$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Kommutative Ringtheorie/Ganzheit/Ganzes Element/Definition -->

### Definition: integral element {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-def-02}

Let $R\subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. An element $x\in S$ is called *integral over $R$* if it satisfies an equation of integral dependence with coefficients in $R$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Kommutative Ringtheorie/Ganzheit/Ganzer Abschluss/Definition -->

### Definition: integral closure {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-def-03}

Let $R\subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. The set of all elements $x\in S$ integral over $R$ is called the *integral closure* of $R$ in $S$.

<!-- upstream_entity: Kommutative Ringtheorie/Ganzheit/Ganze Algebra/Definition -->

### Definition: integral extension {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-def-04}

Let $R\subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. The ring $S$ is called *integral over $R$*, and $R\subseteq S$ an *integral ring extension*, if every $x\in S$ is integral over $R$.

The ring $S$ is integral over $R$ if and only if the integral closure of $R$ in $S$ equals $S$.

### Lemma: characterisation of integral elements {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-lem-01}

Let $R\subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. For an element $x\in S$, the following statements are equivalent.

1. The element $x$ is integral over $R$.
2. There is an $R$-subalgebra $T$ of $S$ containing $x$ that is finitely generated as an $R$-module.
3. There is a finitely generated $R$-submodule $M$ of $S$ containing a non-zero-divisor of $S$ and satisfying $xM\subseteq M$.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-lem-01-proof}

**(1) $\Rightarrow$ (2).** Consider the $R$-subalgebra of $S$ generated by the powers of $x$,

$$
R[x].
$$

This subalgebra consists of all polynomial expressions in $x$ with coefficients in $R$. An equation of integral dependence

$$
x^n+r_{n-1}x^{n-1}+r_{n-2}x^{n-2}+\cdots+r_1x+r_0=0
$$

gives

$$
x^n=-r_{n-1}x^{n-1}-r_{n-2}x^{n-2}-\cdots-r_1x-r_0.
$$

Thus $x^n$ can be expressed as a polynomial expression of smaller degree. Multiplying this last equation by $x^i$ allows every power of $x$ with exponent at least $n$ to be replaced by a polynomial expression of smaller degree. Eventually all such powers can be expressed with degree at most $n-1$. Hence

$$
R[x]=R+Rx+Rx^2+\cdots+Rx^{n-2}+Rx^{n-1},
$$

and $x^0=1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^{n-1}$ form a finite generating system for the $R$-module $T=R[x]$.

**(2) $\Rightarrow$ (3).** Suppose

$$
x\in T\subseteq S,
$$

where $T$ is an $R$-subalgebra finitely generated as an $R$-module. Then $xT\subseteq T$, and $T$ contains the non-zero-divisor $1$.

**(3) $\Rightarrow$ (1).** Let $M\subseteq S$ be a finitely generated $R$-submodule satisfying $xM\subseteq M$, and choose generators $y_1,\ldots,y_n$ for $M$. For every $i$, the element $xy_i$ is an $R$-linear combination of the $y_j$, so

$$
xy_i=\sum_{j=1}^n r_{ij}y_j,
\qquad r_{ij}\in R.
$$

In matrix form,

$$
x
\begin{pmatrix}
y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
r_{1,1}&r_{1,2}&\cdots&r_{1,n}\\
r_{2,1}&r_{2,2}&\cdots&r_{2,n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
r_{n,1}&r_{n,2}&\cdots&r_{n,n}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus

$$
0=
\underbrace{
\begin{pmatrix}
x-r_{1,1}&-r_{1,2}&\cdots&-r_{1,n}\\
-r_{2,1}&x-r_{2,2}&\cdots&-r_{2,n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-r_{n,1}&-r_{n,2}&\cdots&x-r_{n,n}
\end{pmatrix}}_{A}
\begin{pmatrix}
y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entries of the matrix $A$ lie in $S$. If $A^{\operatorname{adj}}$ is the adjugate (classical adjoint) matrix of $A$, then

$$
A^{\operatorname{adj}}Ay=0,
$$

where $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^{\mathsf T}$. By the adjugate identity,

$$
A^{\operatorname{adj}}A=(\det A)I_n,
$$

so

$$
((\det A)I_n)y=0.
$$

Thus $(\det A)y_j=0$ for every $j$, and hence

$$
(\det A)z=0
$$

for every $z\in M$. By hypothesis, $M$ contains a non-zero-divisor of $S$, so $\det A=0$. But this determinant is a monic polynomial expression in $x$ of degree $n$. Thus $x$ satisfies an equation of integral dependence.

### Corollary: integral closure is a subalgebra {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-cor-02}

Let $R\subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. The integral closure of $R$ in $S$ is an $R$-subalgebra of $S$.

#### Proof {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-cor-02-proof}

The equations of integral dependence $X-r=0$, for $r\in R$, show that every element of $R$ is integral over $R$. Let $x_1,x_2\in S$ be integral over $R$. By the characterisation of integrality, there are $R$-subalgebras

$$
T_1,T_2\subseteq S
$$

with $x_1\in T_1$ and $x_2\in T_2$, each finitely generated as an $R$-module. Let $y_1,\ldots,y_n$ be an $R$-generating system for $T_1$ and $z_1,\ldots,z_m$ an $R$-generating system for $T_2$. We may assume $y_1=z_1=1$.

Consider the finitely generated $R$-module

$$
T=T_1T_2
=\left\langle y_iz_j\mathrel{\Big|}
i=1,\ldots,n,\ j=1,\ldots,m\right\rangle.
$$

This module plainly contains $x_1+x_2$, $x_1x_2$, and $1$. The $R$-module $T$ is also an $R$-algebra. Indeed, for two arbitrary elements,

$$
\left(\sum r_{ij}y_iz_j\right)
\left(\sum s_{k\ell}y_kz_\ell\right)
=\sum r_{ij}s_{k\ell}y_iy_kz_jz_\ell,
$$

and $y_iy_k\in T_1$ and $z_jz_\ell\in T_2$, so this linear combination belongs to $T$.

Hence the sum and product of two integral elements are again integral. Thus the integral closure is a subring of $S$ containing $R$, that is, an $R$-subalgebra.

<!-- upstream_entity: Kommutative Ringtheorie/Ganzheit/Ganz-abgeschlossen/Definition -->

### Definition: integrally closed {#br-ak-2025-2026-l19-def-05}

Let $R\subseteq S$ be an extension of commutative rings. The ring $R$ is called *integrally closed in $S$* if the integral closure of $R$ in $S$ equals $R$.
