# Integrative problem 6: Krull dimension, components, and nilpotent thickenings {#d100-bridge-integrative-06}

This problem and solution are independent bridge material. They build on
Holger Brenner's [Lemma 11.3 on prime ideals and irreducible closed
subsets](bgk-reader.html#br-bgk-2019-l11-lem-02),
[Definition 11.6](bgk-reader.html#br-bgk-2019-l11-def-03), and
[Lemma 11.7](bgk-reader.html#br-bgk-2019-l11-lem-04). The calculations
collected here are not attributed as public source solutions.

## Problem {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-soal}

Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field. Consider

$$
L=k[t],\qquad
R=k[x,y]/(xy),\qquad
S=k[t,\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),\qquad
D=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
$$

The variable letters also denote their classes in the quotient rings.
For a nonzero commutative ring $A$, the Krull dimension is the supremum
of the lengths of chains of prime ideals

$$
\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\mathfrak p_1\subsetneq\cdots
\subsetneq\mathfrak p_n;
$$

the length is $n$, the number of strict inclusions, not the number of
ideals. All dimensions in this problem are finite and the suprema are
attained.

1. Determine all prime ideals of each ring and compute its dimension.
   Give both an upper bound and a chain attaining the dimension.
2. Determine the irreducible components and reducedness of all four
   spectra. Explain why $\operatorname{Spec}S$ has the same topology
   as the affine line but is not the same scheme.
3. At the origin, compute the local dimension and
   $\dim_k\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$. The maximal ideals in question
   are respectively $(t)$, $(x,y)$, $(t,\varepsilon)$, and
   $(\varepsilon)$ in their local rings.
4. Compute the generic stalk on the component
   $V(x)\subseteq\operatorname{Spec}R$ and on $\operatorname{Spec}S$.
   Is the generic stalk of an irreducible scheme always a field?

Use that $k[t]$ is a principal ideal domain, by the polynomial division
algorithm. Apart from this fact, prove the required prime ideal and
vector space calculations. For a commutative ring $A$, Lemma 11.7
identifies the dimension of the ring with the dimension of its spectrum;
this affine hypothesis holds in all the examples above.

## Complete solution {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-solusi}

### 1. Prime ideals and chain lengths {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-solusi-01}

In $L=k[t]$, the zero ideal is prime because $L$ is an integral domain.
Nonzero prime ideals are generated by irreducible polynomials. Since $k$
is algebraically closed, these polynomials have degree one, so the ideals
are exactly $(t-a)$ for $a\in k$. They are all maximal, since their
quotient rings are $k$.

Thus no chain of prime ideals has more than one strict inclusion. The
chain $(0)\subsetneq(t)$ attains length one, and $\dim L=1$.

In $R$, the equation $xy=0$ forces every prime ideal to contain $x$ or
$y$. If it contains $x$, it corresponds to a prime ideal in

$$
R/(x)\cong k[y],
$$

and so has the form $(x)$ or $(x,y-b)$, $b\in k$. If it contains $y$,
it has the form $(y)$ or $(y,x-a)$, $a\in k$. This list is complete;
the ideal $(x,y)$ occurs on both lists and is counted only once.

The ideals $(x)$ and $(y)$ are incomparable minimal primes. All other
ideals on the list are maximal. The longest chains therefore have length
one, for example

$$
(x)\subsetneq(x,y),
$$

and $\dim R=1$. Notice that $(0)$ is **not** a prime ideal in $R$:
$x,y$ are nonzero but their product is zero. Thus the chain
$(0)\subsetneq(x)\subsetneq(x,y)$ cannot be used to conclude that
the dimension is two.

In $S$, every prime ideal contains $\varepsilon$, since
$\varepsilon^2=0$. The ideal correspondence for the quotient ring

$$
S/(\varepsilon)\cong k[t]
$$

shows that all prime ideals of $S$ are $(\varepsilon)$ and
$(\varepsilon,t-a)$ for $a\in k$. Hence

$$
(\varepsilon)\subsetneq(\varepsilon,t)
$$

is a maximal chain of length one, and $\dim S=1$.

In $D$, every prime ideal contains $\varepsilon$, and
$D/(\varepsilon)=k$ has only the zero prime ideal. Thus
$(\varepsilon)$ is the only prime ideal of $D$; there are no strict
inclusions in a prime chain, so $\dim D=0$.

### 2. Components and nilpotent elements {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-solusi-02}

The ring $L$ is reduced and has one minimal prime, $(0)$. Its spectrum
is therefore reduced and topologically irreducible. For $R$, the two
irreducible components are $V(x)$ and $V(y)$, each an affine line. The
components meet at $(x,y)$.

Although $R$ has zero divisors, $R$ is still reduced. To prove this, use
the homomorphism

$$
k[x,y]\longrightarrow k[x]\times k[y],\qquad
f\longmapsto(f(x,0),f(0,y)).
$$

Its kernel is $(xy)$. Indeed, if both evaluations are zero, every
remaining monomial in $f$ contains both a factor $x$ and a factor $y$,
so $xy$ divides $f$; the converse is immediate. Thus $R$ embeds into
a product of two integral domains. A nilpotent element of that product
must be zero in both components, so $R$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements.

In contrast, $\varepsilon\ne0$ in both $S$ and $D$, while
$\varepsilon^2=0$. Its nonvanishing follows from the unique expressions
$f(t)+\varepsilon g(t)$ in $S$ and $a+\varepsilon b$ in $D$. Both
rings are nonreduced. Each has one minimal prime, so its spectrum is
topologically irreducible.

The quotient map $S\to S/(\varepsilon)$ gives an inclusion-preserving
bijection of prime ideals. It also identifies closed sets: for an ideal
$I\subseteq S$, all primes containing $I$ already contain
$\varepsilon$, so they correspond exactly to primes in $k[t]$
containing $(I+(\varepsilon))/(\varepsilon)$. Consequently
$\operatorname{Spec}S$ is homeomorphic to $\operatorname{Spec}k[t]$.

They are not isomorphic as schemes. An isomorphism of schemes would
give an isomorphism of global section rings, but $S$ has a nonzero
nilpotent element and $k[t]$ does not. Identifying global sections with
the affine ring uses [Lemma 9.12](bgk-reader.html#br-bgk-2019-l09-lem-03).
Topology and Krull dimension do not see this nilpotent thickening.

### 3. Local dimension and cotangent spaces {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-solusi-03}

The prime ideals of the local ring $A_{\mathfrak m}$ correspond to the
prime ideals of $A$ contained in $\mathfrak m$: contraction and extension
are inverse operations, since the elements outside $\mathfrak m$ have
become units. The lists in part 1 therefore give the local dimensions
at the origin: $1$ for $L,R,S$, and $0$ for $D$.

The residue field at each of the four origins is $k$. To compute
$\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, it suffices to retain the linear terms.
Localisation introduces no further linear relations: modulo the square
of the ideal of the origin, any denominator with value $c\ne0$ can be
written $c+u$ with $u^2=0$, and has inverse

$$
(c+u)^{-1}=c^{-1}-c^{-2}u.
$$

In $L_{(t)}$, the class of $t$ is a basis, so the cotangent dimension is
one. In $R_{(x,y)}$, the maximal ideal is generated by $x,y$, and

$$
R/(x,y)^2\cong k[x,y]/(x^2,xy,y^2).
$$

There is no linear relation between $x$ and $y$ in this quotient ring;
every element has a unique expression $a+bx+cy$. Thus the classes of
$x,y$ form a basis of the two-dimensional cotangent space.

Similarly,

$$
S/(t,\varepsilon)^2
\cong k[t,\varepsilon]/(t^2,t\varepsilon,\varepsilon^2).
$$

The classes of $t,\varepsilon$ are linearly independent and generate
the cotangent space, so its dimension is two. For $D$, the maximal
ideal $(\varepsilon)$ already has square zero and is one-dimensional
over $k$.

The calculations can be summarised as follows. “Cotangent” always
refers to the specified origin.

| Ring | Krull dimension | Local dimension at the origin | Cotangent dimension | Components | Reduced? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| $k[t]$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | Yes |
| $k[x,y]/(xy)$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | Yes |
| $k[t,\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | No |
| $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | No |

Thus Krull dimension is not the number of variables in a presentation,
nor must it equal the cotangent dimension. Even matching Krull and
cotangent dimensions do not distinguish the crossing of two components
from a thickening of a single component.

### 4. Two generic stalks with different properties {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-solusi-04}

The generic point of the component $V(x)$ in $\operatorname{Spec}R$
is the ideal $(x)$. In $R_{(x)}$, the element $y$ is invertible. The
equation $xy=0$ then forces $x=0$. Every nonzero polynomial in $y$
also becomes a unit, so

$$
R_{(x)}\cong k(y).
$$

This is a field, of Krull dimension zero.

The unique generic point of $\operatorname{Spec}S$ is
$\mathfrak p=(\varepsilon)$. Elements outside $\mathfrak p$ have the
form $f(t)+\varepsilon g(t)$ with $f\ne0$. In
$k(t)[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, such an element has inverse

$$
\bigl(f+\varepsilon g\bigr)^{-1}
=f^{-1}-\varepsilon g f^{-2}.
$$

Since all nonzero polynomials $f(t)$ already have to be inverted,
this formula proves

$$
S_{(\varepsilon)}\cong
k(t)[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
$$

This local ring also has dimension zero, but is not a field:
$\varepsilon$ is still nonzero and nilpotent. Thus topological
irreducibility alone does not ensure that the generic stalk is a field.
[Lemma 11.19](bgk-reader.html#br-bgk-2019-l11-lem-10) requires an
*integral* scheme, meaning topologically irreducible and reduced;
$\operatorname{Spec}S$ fails the second condition.

## Checks and common pitfalls {#d100-bridge-integrative-06-periksa}

- To prove a dimension, one long chain gives only a lower bound. The
  complete lists of prime ideals above give the upper bounds.
- In $R$, do not include the zero ideal in a prime chain: $xy=0$ proves
  that it is not prime.
- Reducedness and topological irreducibility are different properties.
  $R$ is reduced but has two components; $S$ has one component but is
  nonreduced.
- The cotangent space uses the square of the maximal ideal, not merely
  the quotient by the maximal ideal. The latter is only the residue
  field and removes all linear classes.
